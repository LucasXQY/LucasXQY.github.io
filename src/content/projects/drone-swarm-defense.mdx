## The task

This was a two-day team entry to the Action Space anti-drone hackathon (October 2026). The
organisers supplied an Isaac Lab simulation kit for DJI Tello drones, the real arena and the
attacking drones; the rules arrived during the event and changed twice. The final version:

- A 6.6 m × 3.5 m room. The base is a 13 cm box 0.86 m out from the wall that carries AprilTag
  10, and the **base line** is 1.30 m out from that wall. An attacker that crosses the line wins.
- Two attacker Tellos take off at the far end of the room, 4.6–6.3 m from the wall, hover for
  1–4 s while they find a tag, then fly at 0.3–0.7 m/s toward the base.
- Three defender Tellos must **physically ram** the attackers from above. The hit box was given
  as 15 × 15 × 3 cm; the simulator reads those as half-extents, so the vertical tolerance is a few
  centimetres either way. A ram takes both drones out of the round, so every defender has exactly
  one shot, and a missed interception must be covered by somebody else.
- A Tello flies about 2 m/s at full stick, accelerates at roughly 3 m/s² and lags its command by
  about 0.25 s. Each drone runs its own policy on its own observation: there is no central
  controller and no channel for the defenders to negotiate who takes whom.

My part of the team's work was the policy side: the simulation changes, the scripted teacher,
the cloning pipeline, the Windows port of the kit and the flight runner. The simulator, the arena,
the attackers and the drone infrastructure (AprilTag localisation, telemetry bus, multi-drone
launcher) came from the organisers. Every number on this page is a simulation result; the page
reports no real-flight results.

## The route in one paragraph

Reinforcement learning on this task did not produce a usable policy in the time available, so
the final pipeline has three stages. A **scripted teacher** flies lead-pursuit interceptions and
decides who chases whom with a small intercept-time table; the drone left over is the goalkeeper.
A **small per-drone network** is cloned from the teacher with DAgger, so that what flies is a
trained policy in a fixed, exportable format. A **flight runner** rebuilds exactly the 34 numbers
the network saw in simulation from the drone's own camera, its teammates and the organisers'
telemetry bus, and turns the network's output into Tello stick commands behind a safety layer.
The one design decision that mattered most was to take the discrete "who chases whom" decision
out of the network and feed it in as an input feature, computed identically on every drone.

## What did not work first

In the week before the event I ran the kit's defence task as shipped, with independent PPO
(IPPO) on an easier two-versus-two curriculum stage (1 m capture sphere, attackers at
0.2–0.4 m/s). The defenders never engaged: they hovered or drifted away. The first finding was not
about rewards at all. The velocity controller computed a world-frame force but applied it as a
body-frame one, so any yaw rotated the horizontal velocity loop and inverted it at 180°; a drone
that turned toward an attacker fled at 3–4 m/s. The scripted baselines never yawed, which is why
they looked fine. After rotating the force into the body frame, IPPO engaged in one of three
seeds and reached 79.2% interception at best, still with 39% of rounds breached; a variant with
less initial action noise engaged in two of three seeds but topped out at 43%.

The obvious fix, warm-starting PPO from a behaviour-cloned copy of the greedy chase baseline
(96% interception on that easy stage before any RL step), failed in a more interesting way: every
PPO continuation decayed monotonically to the level of a stationary policy, across about forty
runs that varied the learning rate, critic fitting, action noise, output bounds and episode
structure. The visible symptom was the policy mean drifting outside the ±1 action clip, where the
clipped log-probabilities distort the gradient; bounding the mean with a tanh slowed the decay but
did not stop it, so the drift was a symptom and the root cause was never found. Once the real
three-versus-two rules arrived, I parked RL and treated the problem as what it is, a geometry
problem with a known good solution, and used learning only to put that solution into a deployable
network.

### Lead pursuit: aim where the attacker will be

Pure pursuit, flying at the attacker's current position, curves in behind a moving target and
arrives from the side. The teacher instead aims at the point where the attacker will be, which
makes the final approach a straight line, and the thin hit box punished every attempt to bend that
line: a vertical P-loop in the last metre missed in 17–28% of attempts and an aim point that slid
down over the last two metres missed in 50–61%, because both lag the Tello's 0.25 s response.
With the attacker at relative position `r`, flying at velocity `v`, and the defender flying straight
at `v_max`, the meeting time solves

```
|r + v t| = v_max t
(|v|² − v_max²) t² + 2 (r · v) t + |r|² = 0
```

Because the attackers are slower than the defenders the quadratic has exactly one positive root,
and the defender flies a straight line to `r + v t`. For an attacker 3 m ahead crossing at
0.6 m/s against a 2 m/s defender, `t` is 1.57 s and the aim point sits 0.94 m to the side of
where the attacker is now. If the equation has no solution the teacher falls back to pure
pursuit. The aim point is biased 2 cm above the attacker so that contact comes from above, and
nothing else touches the terminal run: no slow-down, no height correction, just the straight line.

### Who chases whom: the intercept-time table

The same formula gives the time each defender would need to reach each attacker. Every defender
fills in the 3 × 2 table from its own observation, enumerates the six ways of giving each attacker
a different defender, and takes the pairing with the smallest total time. A spent defender or a
downed attacker costs 1000 s and is never chosen. The defender that is not in the winning pairing
becomes the goalkeeper.

This is decentralised and still consistent, because the table depends only on relative
quantities. When drone 0 asks how long drone 1 needs to reach attacker 1, it subtracts "drone 1
relative to me" from "attacker 1 relative to me", which is exactly "attacker 1 relative to
drone 1", the number drone 1 computes directly. Same inputs, same deterministic code, same
answer on all three drones, with ties broken by a fixed permutation order. Nothing is
communicated except what each drone already observes: the teammates' positions.

The table also replaced an earlier rule that sorted attackers by azimuth around the base and gave
defender _i_ the _i_-th one. That rule failed in exactly one situation, two attackers arriving
within about 15° of each other with one of them fast, where the assigned defender had to cross the
whole room. Measured over 2000 rounds with the 0.20 m contact rule used before the arena was
known:

| Teacher assignment   | interception / breach | mean intercept time | closest pair, min / 5th pct |
| -------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------- | --------------------------- |
| azimuth rank         | 99.9% / 0.1%          | 4.5 s               | 0.20 m / 0.50 m             |
| intercept-time table | **100% / 0%**         | **3.1 s**           | **0.63 m / 1.74 m**         |

Three things improved at once: the last breaches disappeared, interceptions became faster, and
the defenders stopped coming close to each other, because the goalkeeper became a dynamic role
that falls to whichever drone is worst placed.

### The goalkeeper and the "cover" rule

The goalkeeper waits over its own launch spot, about 2 m up and above the attackers' cruising
height. It is dispatched at the attacker nearest the base in two cases: when that attacker is
within 2.2 m of the base and no active teammate is within 1.2 m of it, meaning the planned
interception has failed and nobody is about to fix it; or when the attacker is within 1.7 m
regardless, as a last line. The "no teammate within 1.2 m" condition exists because two defenders
converging on one attacker push each other off the line and both miss.

### Keeping the defenders apart

With teammates in the observation, the teacher adds a short-range repulsion between defenders
(within 0.7 m in the delivered version) and launches the three to slightly staggered heights,
1.45, 1.50 and 1.55 m. The gain is a trade-off: strong horizontal repulsion pushes an interceptor
off its own target when two attackers arrive from similar bearings, and at the strongest setting
tried, interception fell from 100% to 86.5%. Under the earlier contact-sphere rules a mostly
vertical repulsion worked best and lifted the 5th-percentile pair separation from 0.45 m to
0.52 m without losing interceptions; once the thin hit box arrived, any vertical manoeuvre near
the attacker cost hits, so the delivered teacher repels horizontally only and leaves the rest to
the assignment, which keeps the defenders apart in the first place.

## The observation, and why the assignment goes into it

<figure>
  <Image
    src={obsPanel}
    width={720}
    alt="Layout of the 34-dimensional per-drone observation: 9 ego values, 2 attackers by 7 values, 2 teammates by 4 values, and a 3-value assignment feature holding a one-hot target and a goalkeeper flag"
  />
  <figcaption>
    What each drone sees at every step. The last three numbers are the decision the network does not
    have to make.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Each defender observes 34 numbers: its own position relative to the base, velocity and body-frame
gravity (9); for each attacker the relative position, relative velocity and an alive flag (2 × 7);
for each teammate the relative position and an active flag (2 × 4); and the assignment feature
(3): a one-hot of the attacker it is responsible for, and a goalkeeper flag. The action is four
numbers in [−1, 1], a world-frame velocity and a yaw rate, which the flight runner rotates by the
drone's heading into the Tello's four stick channels.

The assignment feature is the heart of the project. A direct clone of the intercept-time teacher,
with the first 31 numbers only, reached 98.2% interception with 3.4% of rounds breached and a
direction cosine of 0.95 to the teacher. The reason is structural: the assignment is the argmin of
six sums, and near a tie a tiny change in positions flips which attacker is "mine". A network is a
smooth function and blurs that cliff, so near ties it flies at neither attacker properly. Moving
the argmin into the observation, computed by the same deterministic routine in the simulator, in
the teacher and later in the flight runner, left the network with only continuous skills to learn:
pursue, avoid, hold, return.

| Clone of the same teacher       | assignment in the input | interception / breach | direction cosine |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------- |
| 31-dim observation              | no                      | 98.2% / 3.4%          | 0.95             |
| 34-dim observation, 2000 rounds | yes                     | **100% / 0%**         | 0.996–0.998      |

The two rows differ in nothing but those three input numbers.

## Distilling the teacher with DAgger

<figure>
  <Image
    src={daggerPanel}
    width={720}
    alt="DAgger loop: roll out the teacher with Gaussian noise and label with the clean teacher, fit the network by mean squared error, roll out the student and relabel every state with the teacher, merge the data and refit the observation normaliser, repeat, then export TorchScript"
  />
  <figcaption>The cloning loop. The exported network carries its own input normaliser.</figcaption>
</figure>

Why not fly the teacher itself? The task statement asked for a policy trained in simulation; the
teacher reads the simulator's configuration objects and is written for thousands of parallel
environments on a GPU; and a small network is a fixed contract, 34 numbers in and 4 out, that
exports to TorchScript or plain numpy arrays. Each defender's network is a multilayer perceptron,
34 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 4, about fifty thousand parameters, with the observation normaliser folded
into the exported module.

Plain behaviour cloning fails here for the usual reason, compounding error. The teacher never
drifts, so a student that imitates it never sees what a slightly-off state looks like; its own
small errors carry it into states it has no labels for, where it errs more. Off-line cloning of the first scripted teacher reached only 32–47% interception, even with a
generous 0.35 m capture sphere (that teacher also used a teammate distance a single drone cannot
observe, which is what led to the observation-only assignment above). DAgger closes the gap by letting the student fly and asking the teacher to label every state the student
actually visits:

```
round 0 : fly the teacher with Gaussian action noise (DART, σ = 0.3),
          label every state with the clean teacher action
round k : fly the student (mean action),
          label every visited state with the teacher,
          add to the dataset, refit the normaliser,
          refit the network (MSE to the teacher's action)
```

The delivered clone used ten DAgger rounds of 800 steps × 512 environments, about 384k samples
per drone per round and 4.2 million by the end, 60 epochs per refit, roughly forty minutes on a
laptop RTX 5080. Hold-out error ended around 0.0005 with a direction cosine of 0.999 to the
teacher. Two DAgger rounds had already taken the cosine from 0.974 to 0.992 on the first
three-versus-two version; four rounds matched the teacher. Distillation does not improve on the
teacher, it preserves the teacher in a form that can fly.

## Results

The delivered policy was evaluated over 1000 rounds in the final arena geometry, with the take-off
phase, the hover-and-search of the attackers, the 15 × 15 × 3 cm hit box and the "both drones out
after a ram" rule:

| Final arena, 1000 rounds      | interception / breach | rams by defender 0 / 1 / 2 | mean intercept time | pairs closer than 0.30 m |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------ |
| scripted teacher (300 rounds) | 100% / 0%             | 1.00 / 0.19 / 0.81         | 4.7 s               | 1.0%                     |
| **cloned network**            | **99.7% / 0.1%**      | 0.99 / 0.18 / 0.82         | 4.8 s               | 1.2%                     |

The one breach in a thousand is the second attacker leaking through after the first ram, with both
attackers near their speed limit. The previous clone, launched from 2 m with eight rounds and 40
epochs, breached 1.2% of rounds, all in one pattern: after the first ram the two remaining
defenders crowded the second attacker and both missed. The longer cloning schedule alone, still
from 2 m, brought breaches down to 0.3%; with the 1.5 m launch heights they fell to 0.1% and the
close-pair rounds from 4.0% to 1.2%. For scale, the greedy chase baseline under the earlier
contact-sphere rules intercepted 86.8% of attackers and let 23.5% of rounds breach; the last clip
below shows why.

## From simulation to the real drones

<figure>
  <Image
    src={runnerPanel}
    width={720}
    alt="One control tick of the flight runner for drone i: telemetry-bus attackers, AprilTag ego pose and in-process teammates are combined into the 34-dimensional observation, the network produces the action, a safety layer applies a 0.6 m separation guard and inactivity rules, and the world-frame velocity is rotated by the heading into Tello rc channels"
  />
  <figcaption>One control tick on the real drone, 13.5 times per second.</figcaption>
</figure>

The network only knows the world it saw in simulation, so the runner's job is to make the real
world produce the same 34 numbers with the same meaning, coordinate frame and clipping, and to
keep everything that is not in the simulation outside the network, in plain code with tests.

- **Own pose** comes from the drone's camera: AprilTag detection against the organisers' room
  map, the same localiser their tools use. Velocity is a 0.4 s finite difference.
- **Attackers** come from the organisers' telemetry bus, where their attacking policy publishes
  each drone's position and state. An attacker counts as alive while its message is younger than
  1 s, it reads above 50 cm, and its state is not landing or done; a rammed or landed attacker
  keeps its last position with the alive flag at zero, exactly the simulator's convention.
- **Teammates** are the other defenders in the same process. A teammate is marked inactive when
  it has been below 30 cm for a second, silent for three seconds or without a fix for six, which is
  what "spent after a ram" looks like on real hardware.
- **The assignment feature** is a line-by-line numpy port of the simulator's routine, checked
  equal to the torch original on 400 random observations for every drone.
- **Output**: the network's world-frame velocity is clipped by a room fence, rotated by the
  drone's heading into body-frame stick values, and sent as the Tello `rc` command. The yaw
  channel is overridden to keep the camera on the tags.
- **Safety layer**: a climb phase to the launch heights (145/150/155 cm) before the network takes
  over; a hold when no attacker is on the bus, since the network never saw an empty field; a
  separation guard that makes the higher-numbered defender hold horizontally when two come within
  0.6 m until they are 0.8 m apart; hover after 0.5 s without a position fix and land after 6 s.
- **Two copies of the weights**: TorchScript for a laptop with PyTorch, and the same networks as
  numpy arrays for one without. On 500 random inputs the two agree to 4 × 10⁻⁶.

The runner ships as a policy class inside the organisers' multi-drone launcher, with a
configuration file, 24 unit tests and a no-drone kinematic demo, so that they could run it next to
their attackers without changing anything else in their stack.

## What I would say about it

The pieces are classical: lead pursuit is interception geometry, the assignment is a tiny
instance of the assignment problem solved by enumeration, DAgger and DART are published
imitation-learning methods. The contribution is in how they fit together under the real rules:
a decentralised assignment that every drone reproduces from its own observation; the finding,
with a one-variable ablation, that the discrete decision must be an **input** to the network
rather than something it learns; and the engineering that keeps simulation, teacher and flight
runner numerically consistent, down to a numpy twin of the weights.

Three lessons I am keeping. Debug the controller before the reward: days of "the policy is
passive" were a frame bug. When a learned policy must make a discrete choice, compute the choice
and hand it over, and let the network do what it is good at. And when the rules keep changing,
a scripted teacher plus distillation turns a rule change into a forty-minute re-clone instead of
a new training run.
