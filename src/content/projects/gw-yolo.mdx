## The problem

Gravitational-wave detectors record strain, a measure of how a passing wave stretches and squeezes their arms. On a Q-transform time–frequency image of that record, the chirp from a merging pair of compact objects traces a rising track that a YOLO-based object detector can find and box. Glitches, transient noise artifacts that arise inside a single detector, can look much like those chirps. An object detector also returns boxes in one detector at a time, while a search needs the evidence from two detectors ranked by a single statistic, with a false-alarm rate measured on real noise.

## The idea

This project builds that statistic on top of the boxes from the LIGO Hanford and Livingston detectors. It scores each candidate from two kinds of evidence: whether the boxes in the two detectors coincide in time, since a gravitational wave reaches both while a glitch stays local to one, and whether the strain inside the boxes agrees between the detectors. The statistic has no trainable parameters, and its background is estimated from time slides of real detector noise.

## How it is tested

Each evaluation was registered before its test data were chosen, with the criteria and the predicted outcomes written down first, and was then run once. The study covers two periods of LIGO's fourth observing run and a set of catalogued binary-black-hole events, and compares the statistic with a plain time-coincidence baseline on the same boxes. Predictions that failed are reported next to those that held. A manuscript is in preparation, so the numbers stay out of this page for now.

## Why it matters

A detection method is only useful to a search if its false-alarm rate is known and its reported performance can be trusted. Fixing the analysis before seeing the test data removes the freedom to tune after the fact. The low-light work in [VetoNet](/projects/vetonet/) rests on the same principle: claim only what the measurements support.
